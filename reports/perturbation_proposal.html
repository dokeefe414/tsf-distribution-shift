Research plan • October 2026

Perturbation Response of Time-Series Forecasters

A controlled experimental study of input sensitivity, forecasting accuracy, and extensions toward information geometry.

Concrete first experiment Researcher: Daniel O'Keefe

1. Motivation and research question

Previous experiments compared ARIMA and Kalman forecasting methods on synthetic series with changing regimes. Those results are useful but do not isolate which particular change caused a forecast response. The next stage studies controlled modifications to the historical input of a fixed forecasting model.

Core question: How does a trained one-step Transformer-style forecaster respond to small Gaussian perturbations of its historical observations? How does the response vary with perturbation variance, and is sensitivity associated with reduced forecasting accuracy?

2. Mathematical problem formulation

Let a generator G produce a discrete real-valued time series x[t]. Given the previous L observations X[t]=(x[t-L], ..., x[t-1]), a trained forecaster Fθ returns a one-step estimate of x[t].

clean prediction = Fθ(X[t])

Introduce random input perturbations ε drawn from a controlled distribution Pσ. In the first experiment, they are zero-mean, independent Gaussian variables:

ε ~ N(0, σ² I[L]); X'[t] = X[t] + ε
response Δ[t,ε] = Fθ(X'[t]) - Fθ(X[t])

Measure the mean absolute response over test samples and repeated noise draws. Evaluate forecast RMSE against original unperturbed targets separately. Keep θ fixed during the perturbation experiment.

3. Generator G: proposed distribution collection

Choose the simplest distribution that can expose a specific behavior, then increase complexity only after a control experiment works.

DistributionJustificationOrder
Stationary Gaussian AR(1)Known conditional mean, controlled covariance, analytically interpretable linear baselineFirst
Gaussian with mean/variance changeSeparates level shifts from volatility shifts using controlled interventionsNext
Correlated multivariate GaussianAllows directional perturbations and covariance eigenvector experimentsNext
LaplaceHeavier tails probe reactions to occasional larger changesLater
UniformBounded-control comparison, though sharp density boundaries complicate directional-score analysisLater
Gaussian mixtureIntroduces multimodality and distinct density regionsLater

5. Perturbation fidelity and response directions

Small perturbation variance controls the per-coordinate change but does not automatically preserve the joint temporal distribution. With L dimensions and isotropic normalized Gaussian noise, E||ε||² = Lσ². In the next stage, compare directions by perturbing along individual input coordinates, shared all-one directions, and covariance eigenvectors. Track changes in input covariance and measure response anisotropy.

A directional score (gradient of log probability density) is available analytically for a Gaussian distribution with invertible covariance. This can motivate density-aligned perturbation directions later; it is not part of the initial results.

6. Training choices and objectives

Investigate how window length L, training random seed, regularization and model size affect sensitivity. Begin with pointwise MSE and AdamW. Future objectives may include distributional losses for ensembles and time-alignment losses for longer-horizon prediction; such objectives require careful definition and are not implemented initially.

7. Longer-horizon and information-geometric extensions

After one-step experiments are validated, use autoregressive rollouts to study propagation of small input changes through many steps. For statistical-manifold studies, use parameterized distribution families (e.g. Gaussian mean and covariance paths) and potentially Fisher-information-based local distances. The current study does not compute geodesics or Riemannian geometry.

8. Interpretable surrogate and fidelity

A future surrogate S can approximate the Transformer F. Assess both output fidelity |S(X)-F(X)| and response fidelity |(S(X+ε)-S(X))-(F(X+ε)-F(X))|. They need not coincide. The meaning of the advisor's abbreviated “mutual D2” objective should be clarified before formalizing a joint optimization loss.

9. Validity, limitations and deliverables

  • Compare the Transformer to a linear AR(1) benchmark appropriate for the generator.
  • Keep training/validation/test targets disjoint; fit scaling using training observations only.
  • Retrain using several seeds and quantify both training variation and perturbation variation.
  • Distinguish output sensitivity, true forecast deterioration, and a shift in the underlying generator.
  • Document scripts, configuration, CSV metrics, plots and any failure modes.

Deliverables: HTML proposal, AR(1) data generator, custom documented Transformer, perturbation evaluation script, plots and tables, initial interpretation, and future correlated perturbation and long-horizon extensions.

This is a working research proposal, not a claim that every proposed extension has already been completed.
